I oppose the motion.

Strict laws to regulate LLMs would be a mistake because they would punish the technology itself instead of targeting actual misuse. The problem is not that LLMs exist; it is that some people use tools irresponsibly. We do not ban pens because people write lies, and we should not strangle LLMs with heavy-handed laws because bad actors can exploit them.

First, strict regulation would choke innovation. LLMs are still a rapidly evolving field, and rigid legal rules would favor only the biggest corporations that can afford compliance teams and lawyers. That means less competition, fewer startups, and slower progress. In practice, strict regulation often protects incumbents, not the public.

Second, broad laws are usually too blunt for a technology this flexible. LLMs can be used for education, accessibility, coding, translation, research, and customer service. If you overregulate the model itself, you risk limiting all those benefits because of a few misuse cases. The answer should be targeted enforcement against fraud, defamation, and impersonation—not blanket restrictions on the underlying tool.

Third, strict laws create a false sense of safety. Bureaucratic compliance does not guarantee trustworthy AI. Bad outputs, bias, and hallucinations will not disappear because a law says they should. What actually improves safety is better testing, transparency, user controls, and market competition pushing companies to build better systems.

Finally, strict regulation is outdated by the time it is written. LLM technology changes too quickly for rigid laws to keep up. By the time legislation is passed, it may already be obsolete or misaligned with the newest models. Flexible standards, industry best practices, and case-by-case accountability are far more effective.

So the choice is not between regulation and chaos. The choice is between smart, targeted oversight and rigid laws that slow progress, entrench monopolies, and miss the real problem. We should regulate misuse, not suffocate innovation.
